## Getting the blood back

When an artery is blocked, the only way to stop more muscle from dying is to get blood flowing again. Bringing blood back is called **[reperfusion](term:reperfusion)**.

In hospital, doctors push a thin tube up to the blockage, open it with a tiny balloon and usually leave a metal mesh tube, a stent, to hold it open: a **[PCI](term:pci)**. Where that is not possible quickly, a clot-dissolving drug can be given instead.

In the lab, the artery is tied off for a set time and then the tie is released, or the blood flow to the whole heart is stopped and then restarted.

::scene{camera="short-axis" highlight="infarct" anim="reperfusion"}

## Time is muscle

Module 5 showed that muscle dies from the inner wall outward, over hours. Reperfusion stops that **[wavefront](term:wavefront)** wherever it has got to. Muscle that was still alive in the outer wall is saved.

So the earlier the artery is opened, the more muscle survives. Doctors say **time is muscle**: every half hour of delay costs a little more of the heart.

:::quiz{type="multiple-choice" id="m6-when"}
An artery is blocked. When should it be reopened to save the most muscle?
- [x] As soon as possible
- [ ] After a few hours, once the muscle has rested
- [ ] It makes no difference, as long as it is reopened the same day
> The dead zone keeps growing until the blood comes back, so every minute saved saves muscle.
:::

## The second injury

Reperfusion is not all good news. The returning blood brings oxygen back with a rush, and in the first minutes some cells that had survived the blockage are pushed over the edge:

- oxygen reacting with the damaged cells makes harmful molecules, **free radicals**;
- the cells take in too much calcium, so they cramp and their energy factories, the mitochondria, break open;
- inflammation follows.

This **[reperfusion injury](term:reperfusion-injury)** is thought to cause up to half of the final infarct. Reperfusion is still essential, but the injury it adds is a target for new treatments.

:::quiz{type="true-false" answer="true" id="m6-harm"}
Restoring blood flow saves muscle overall, but can also kill some cells that survived the blockage.
> Both are true: reperfusion stops the infarct growing, but the rush of oxygen and calcium kills some cells as it arrives. That is reperfusion injury.
:::

## Protecting the heart

In 1986 researchers found something surprising: a few short blockages of a few minutes each, before the long one, made the final infarct much smaller. The heart had been warned. This is **[ischemic preconditioning](term:preconditioning)**. Short blockages given right after reperfusion, called postconditioning, can protect too.

Since then, many drugs and procedures have been tried to protect the heart in the same way. To know whether one works, researchers compare the **infarct size** in treated and untreated hearts, which is exactly what TTC staining and Cardia measure. Module 7 explains why that number matters so much.

## In the lab's records

A lab experiment is described by two times, which Cardia keeps with every heart:

- **ischemia**: how long the blood flow was stopped;
- **reperfusion**: how long it ran again before the heart was stained.

The reperfusion time matters for TTC too: muscle that died only minutes before staining can still hold enough enzyme to turn red, so protocols allow enough reperfusion, often an hour or two, before staining.
