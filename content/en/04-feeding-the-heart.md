## The heart's own blood supply

The heart pumps blood all day, yet it cannot feed on the blood inside its chambers: the wall is too thick for oxygen to soak through. Instead, the muscle is fed by its own arteries running over its surface, the **[coronary arteries](term:coronary-arteries)**. They are named after the Latin for crown, because they circle the heart like one.

The first two branches the **[aorta](part:aorta)** gives off, just above the aortic valve, are the coronary arteries. So the heart feeds itself before anything else.

:::quiz{type="true-false" answer="false" id="m4-chambers"}
Heart muscle takes its oxygen from the blood passing through its chambers.
> The wall is too thick for that. The muscle is fed by its own arteries, the coronary arteries, which branch off the aorta.
:::

## Three main arteries

- The **[right coronary artery](part:rca)** (RCA) runs in the groove between the right atrium and the right ventricle, round to the back of the heart.
- The left coronary artery is short. It splits almost at once into two:
  - the **[left anterior descending artery](part:lad)** (LAD), which runs down the front of the heart, over the septum, towards the apex;
  - the **[left circumflex artery](part:lcx)** (LCx), which wraps round the left side in the groove below the left atrium.

Each gives off smaller branches. The LAD's branches feed the front of the left ventricle and most of the septum; the circumflex's feed its side wall.

## Each artery's patch

Each artery feeds its own patch of muscle, its **[territory](term:territory)**. Roughly:

- the **[LAD's territory](part:lad-territory)** is the front wall of the left ventricle, the front of the septum and the apex, the largest of the three;
- the **[circumflex's territory](part:lcx-territory)** is the left ventricle's side wall;
- the **[RCA's territory](part:rca-territory)** is the right ventricle and, in most people, the bottom of the left ventricle and the back of the septum.

The exact borders differ from person to person and between species. Clinicians use a standard map of 17 segments of the left ventricle to say which artery probably feeds which part.

## Fed between beats

Most of the blood reaches the left ventricle's muscle while the heart relaxes, in [diastole](term:diastole). When the ventricle squeezes, it squeezes its own small vessels shut, especially those deep in the wall.

That makes the **[inner layer](part:endocardium)** of the wall, next to the chamber, the hardest part to feed: it is farthest from the arteries on the surface and is squeezed the hardest. When blood flow falls, the inner layer runs short of oxygen first. Module 5 shows what happens next.

## Back-up routes, and why the species matters

Some hearts have extra small vessels linking one artery's territory to another's: **[collaterals](term:collateral)**. If one artery is blocked, blood can sneak in through them from a neighbour, and less muscle dies.

How many collaterals a heart has differs a lot between species. Guinea pigs have so many that blocking one artery can leave little or no infarct; dogs have some; rats, rabbits and pigs have very few. That is one reason the same experiment can give very different infarcts in different species, why many guinea pig studies stop the blood flow to the whole heart instead of one artery, and why a lab must compare like with like.
