## The number that matters most

Of everything that can be measured after a heart attack, the size of the dead area, the **[infarct size](term:infarct-size)**, is one of the strongest predictors of what happens next. Dead heart muscle does not grow back: it becomes scar, and the heart has less muscle left to pump with.

In patients, a bigger infarct means a higher risk of heart failure and of death in the following year. A large study that pooled ten clinical trials found the risk rising steadily with every few percent of extra infarct.

:::quiz{type="true-false" answer="true" id="m7-outcome"}
Patients with larger infarcts are more likely to develop heart failure.
> Yes. With less living muscle left, the heart pumps less well, and infarct size is one of the strongest predictors of what happens next.
:::

## A test for new treatments

That is why infarct size is the main result in studies of treatments meant to protect the heart. Researchers block an artery, or the whole heart's blood flow, in animals that got the treatment and in animals that did not, then compare how much muscle died.

If the treated hearts have smaller infarcts, the treatment may protect the heart. The difference between groups is often only a few [percentage points](term:percentage-points), so the measurement has to be precise.

## Out of what?

A percentage needs a "per": an infarct of 40% means 40% of what? Studies use different answers:

- of the left ventricle, where almost all infarcts happen;
- of the whole heart or of all the slices, when the blood flow to the whole heart was stopped;
- of the **[area at risk](term:area-at-risk)**, the muscle the blocked artery fed. This corrects for one animal's artery feeding more muscle than another's.

Cardia reports the infarct as a percentage of the tissue in the slices, combined by [slice weight](term:slice-weight). When you compare numbers from different studies, always check what the percentage is of.

:::quiz{type="multiple-choice" id="m7-aar"}

- [ ] TTC cannot show the whole heart
> Arteries feed different amounts of muscle in different animals. Dividing by the area at risk compares like with like.
:::

## Why precision saves animals

Every measurement has some error: two people tracing the same slice draw slightly different borders, and the same person draws differently on different days. That noise hides real differences between groups.

The noisier the measurement, the more animals a study needs to see a real effect. A consistent, automatic measurement, the same scan always giving the same number, means fewer animals for the same answer. That is one of the reasons Cardia exists (module 11).

## Lost in translation

Many treatments that shrank infarcts in animals failed when they were tried in patients. The reasons include differences between species and models, small studies, and measurements that were not blinded or standardised.

In response, research groups now share stricter guidelines and run multi-lab studies with central, blinded measurement of infarct size. Careful, reproducible measurement is not a detail: it decides which treatments reach patients.
