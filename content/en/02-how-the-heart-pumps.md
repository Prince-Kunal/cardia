## One beat, in two halves

Every heartbeat is the same short sequence, repeated about 60 to 100 times a minute in a resting adult, and three to four times as fast in a guinea pig.

First the two **[atria](term:atrium)** squeeze, then the two **[ventricles](term:ventricle)**. Then everything relaxes and fills again. One full round, from one beat to the next, is the **[cardiac cycle](term:cardiac-cycle)**.

The squeezing part is called **[systole](term:systole)**; the relaxing and filling part is **[diastole](term:diastole)**.

::scene{camera="section" highlight="tricuspid,mitral" anim="heartbeat" speed=0.5}

## Filling: the doors between the chambers are open

While the ventricles relax, the valves between atria and ventricles, the **[tricuspid](part:tricuspid)** on the right and the **[mitral](part:mitral)** on the left, hang open. Blood coming back to the heart flows through the atria and straight on into the ventricles.

Most of the filling happens like this, on its own. At the end, the atria squeeze and push in the last part, about a fifth of the total at rest.

:::quiz{type="true-false" answer="false" id="m2-muscles-open"}
Small muscles pull the heart's valves open and shut.
> No muscle opens a valve. Valves are flaps that blood pushes: they open when the pressure behind them is higher, and shut when it reverses.
:::

::scene{camera="section" highlight="pulmonary-valve,aortic-valve" anim="heartbeat" speed=0.5}

## Squeezing: lub, then dub

When the ventricles start to squeeze, the pressure inside them jumps above the pressure in the atria. The tricuspid and mitral valves slam shut, so blood cannot go back. That closing is the first heart sound, the **lub**.

A moment later the pressure is higher than in the arteries, and the **[pulmonary valve](part:pulmonary-valve)** and **[aortic valve](part:aortic-valve)** are pushed open. Blood rushes out: from the right ventricle to the lungs, from the left ventricle to the body.

When the ventricles relax, the pressure falls below the arteries' and those two valves snap shut: the second sound, the **dub**.

:::quiz{type="order-steps" id="m2-beat"}
Put one heartbeat in order, starting with the heart relaxed.
1. Blood flows through the atria into the ventricles
2. The atria squeeze in the last of the blood
3. The ventricles squeeze and the tricuspid and mitral valves shut
4. The pulmonary and aortic valves open and blood leaves the heart
5. The ventricles relax and the pulmonary and aortic valves shut
:::

## What starts each beat

Heart muscle starts its own beats: no signal from the brain is needed. A small patch of special cells in the wall of the **[right atrium](part:ra)**, the **[sinoatrial node](term:sinoatrial-node)**, fires an electrical signal again and again. It is the heart's pacemaker.

The signal spreads across both atria, so they squeeze together. It is then held back for a split second at the junction with the ventricles, which gives the atria time to finish emptying. Finally it races down the septum and spreads through the ventricles from the apex upward, so they squeeze from the bottom up, like squeezing toothpaste from the end of the tube.

The brain and hormones only speed this pacemaker up or slow it down.

:::quiz{type="fill-blank" id="m2-pacemaker"}

:::

## Why the left side works harder

Both ventricles pump the same amount of blood with every beat. But the **[left ventricle](part:lv)** pushes it all round the body, against a high pressure, while the **[right ventricle](part:rv)** only pushes it through the nearby lungs, at about a fifth of that pressure.

That is why the left ventricle's wall is about three times as thick as the right's, and why it needs so much oxygen. When its blood supply fails, it suffers most: that is the subject of modules 4 and 5.
